The election in Ramapur had never mattered this much.

For years the village picked its sarpanch without much fuss. Everyone knew who would stand, who would win, and how little would change after. This time two men wanted the chair, and one of them had worked out a very simple way to get it.

Ravi farmed two acres near the canal. Born in the village, likely to die in it. Honest, hardworking, no good at talking. Suresh was good at talking. He could make a man feel he had finally been understood.

A week before the vote Suresh came to the banyan tree by Kishan's tea stall, where the farmers sat out the hot part of the afternoon.

"How long has this water business gone on now? Eleven years?"

"Twelve," somebody said.

"Twelve. Make me sarpanch and the canal is repaired before planting."

Heads went up and down.

That evening he was on the main road with the shopkeepers, telling them the weekly market belonged in Ramapur and had no business being held in Chintalapalli, and that the road would be tarred. More people, more sales. They liked it. Bhaskar, who ran the fertiliser shop, shook his hand twice.

Next morning, the boys near the cricket ground. No reason for any of you to go to Hyderabad for work, he said. Training will come here. I know people in the companies.

They listened. Some of them had already packed once.

By the end of that week most of Ramapur had heard something from Suresh they wanted to believe. Streetlights and taps that ran past eight in the morning. The temple pond cleaned out. Two rupees more on a litre of milk.

Nobody put it together that he had told each of them a different thing.

Ravi put it together.

One evening he found Suresh alone at the stall, a glass in front of him.

"You are promising a lot."

Suresh smiled. "That is what an election is."

"And when they come asking?"

"I will do what I can."

"The rest?"

Suresh drank.

"Something will happen."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the money won't come from the district. Or the rain fails. Or the file sits with some clerk in Nizamabad for a year. Or the sanction is half of what was asked. Take your pick. There is always a reason."

Ravi looked at him.

"You know you cannot do half of what you are saying."

"I know something better than that." Suresh set the glass down. "If I tell Rangaiah today that I cannot fix his canal, he will remember it on voting day. If I promise and fail in six months, he will curse the government, the officials, the rain, the last sarpanch. Or he will say such work takes time."

He smiled. "And by then it is finished. The voting, I mean."

They stood in line in the sun on polling day. Old women under pallus, boys on their phones. Most had decided weeks back. A few were still chewing over what they had been told.

The counting finished by nine that night. Suresh won by a hundred and forty votes.

Drums outside his house. Sweets. People pushing in to say they had known all along.

Three weeks of quiet.

Then the asking started.

Farmers first. The canal, before planting, he had said it under the banyan tree in front of eleven men. Of course, Suresh said. It is in hand.

Then the shopkeepers about the road. Gone to the mandal office, he said. Waiting for approval.

The boys about the training. In progress. These things take time.

Water, he said, pipeline has been proposed. Milk rates, he said, I have spoken to the officials.

Nothing he said was unreasonable. Nothing he said was a lie exactly. And nothing happened.

The planting came and went. The canal stayed broken and they pumped from the borewell until it started coughing air. The road kept its potholes and Bhaskar's delivery man cracked an axle on one. No training. Taps the same as always.

One afternoon Ravi was under the banyan again. Rangaiah lowered himself onto the platform beside him, slow, one hand on his knee.

"You remember what he stood there and said?"

"I remember."

"Now it is the government's fault. The money has not come." Rangaiah laughed, one short sound with nothing good in it.

Neither of them said anything for a while. A bus went past toward the main road and the dust took its time settling.

"Why did we believe him?"

Ravi thought about it.

"Because he told each of us the one thing we wanted to hear. Not the same thing. Ours."

A month later they announced elections for the farmers' cooperative. President's post.

Suresh started his rounds again. One thing for the farmers, another for the traders, something else entirely for the younger members.

People listened. They nodded. Rangaiah even smiled at him and asked after his mother.

Then Suresh walked off toward the next house, and the old man watched him go, and spat into the dust.

"Once he fooled us with his mouth. Now he has to fool what we remember also."

Ravi did not say anything. But he was still sitting there smiling when the light went.
